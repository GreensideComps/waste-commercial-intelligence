# Bias Controls and Interpretation Rules

You are interviewing people who know you, about an idea you have spent weeks on,
using leads you found yourself. **Every structural bias in qualitative research
is working against you at once.** This document exists to stop you fooling
yourself.

---

## 1. The seven biases, and the specific control for each

### 1.1 Leading questions
**The risk:** "Wouldn't it be useful to know about this earlier?" contains its
own answer.

**Controls**
- Q1 is closed and categorical (A/B/C/D), asked before any discussion.
- Never use "useful", "valuable", "helpful" or "opportunity" in a question until
  the participant has used the word first.
- ❌ "Would this help you win work?" → ✅ "Would NRS pursue this? Why?"
- ❌ "This wouldn't normally show up in Barbour, would it?" → ✅ "Would you have
  expected to find this through your existing sources?"

### 1.2 Anchoring
**The risk:** the moment they know what you think a lead is worth, or which ones
you rate, every subsequent answer moves toward it.

**Controls**
- **The Side 1 / Side 2 card fold.** Relevance and confidence are physically
  hidden until awareness is recorded.
- **No scores, no rankings, no tonnages, no £ values on any card.**
- **They name the price first** (Q3.8). If you name a number, that number is the
  answer you get back, and the WTP data is void.
- Card order is pre-shuffled and fixed.

### 1.3 Confirmation bias (yours, not theirs)
**The risk:** you hear "that's interesting" as validation and write down
"interested".

**Controls**
- Record the **letter** (A/B/C/D) and **verbatim quotes**, not impressions.
- The four-part actionability test is a checklist, not a judgement.
- The post-interview note asks explicitly: *"Anything you said that you
  shouldn't have?"*
- **Write the capture sheet during the interview, not from memory afterwards.**

### 1.4 Social desirability
**The risk:** people are kind. They will not tell a man who spent three weeks on
something that it is worthless.

**Controls**
- The opening line does the heavy lifting: *"The most useful answer is 'we knew
  that' … if you're polite about it, you'll cost me money."*
- Two disconfirming questions at the close (Q3.11, Q3.12) give explicit
  permission to be negative.
- Card 10 (Egghill) is deliberately weak. **If a participant is enthusiastic
  about Card 10, they are being agreeable rather than analytical — discount
  their whole interview.** It functions as a politeness detector, the way Card 7
  functions as a knowledge detector.

### 1.5 The Ben effect 🔴 the hardest one
**The risk:** these people know you, may like you, and some may have a
commercial relationship with you. Their answers are systematically inflated and
**you cannot fully remove this.**

**Controls, in descending order of effectiveness**
1. **Revealed preference over stated preference.** Q3.13 plus 14 days of silence
   is worth more than every verbal answer combined.
2. **Have someone else run at least one interview** if at all possible. If the
   answers from that interview are materially more negative, apply that gap as a
   correction to the others.
3. **Ask for a budget displacement** (Q3.10) — "what would you stop spending on"
   is much harder to answer politely than "would you pay".
4. **Discount all positive verbal signals by default.** Treat "yes we'd pay" as
   neutral, not positive, unless a number and a budget holder are named.

### 1.6 "That's interesting" ≠ commercially actionable 🔴
**The risk:** the single most common false positive in customer discovery.
"Interesting" is what people say to be agreeable while committing to nothing.

**Control: the four-part test in `data-collection.md`.** A lead is actionable
only if the participant supplies **why, who, what and when**, unprompted. If you
have to feed them any of the four, it fails. **"That's interesting" scores
zero.** Write the word "INTERESTING" in the comments column so you can count how
often it happens — a high count is itself a finding.

### 1.7 Recall and hindsight bias
**The risk:** "Oh yes, I think I'd seen that" — people over-report prior
knowledge to appear well-informed, which biases *against* your idea.

**Control:** Q2 requires the **channel and rough date**. If they cannot say how
they knew, downgrade B to A and note the uncertainty. Apply this rule
consistently, including when it helps you — the point is accuracy, not
advantage.

## 2. Rules for interpreting answers

| What they say | What it means | Score it as |
|---|---|---|
| "That's interesting" (nothing more) | Politeness | **Not actionable** |
| "I didn't know that" + names a contact + a timeframe | Real | **Unknown + actionable** |
| "We know the site but not that it was happening now" | **Category C — the key finding** | **Unknown** |
| "We'd definitely look at that" (no who, no when) | Enthusiasm without commitment | Not actionable |
| "Send me that one" | Mild positive | Log for the 14-day follow-up |
| "I'll call them this week" | **Strong** | Actionable; verify at 14 days |
| "Yeah we'd pay for that" (no number) | **Worthless** | Not a WTP signal |
| "About £500 a month, out of the marketing budget" | Real | **WTP signal** |
| "You'd have to talk to [name] about budget" | Deflection, but honest | Half signal — follow up |
| "We get that from Barbour already" | Direct competitive answer | Duplicate — record the source |
| Long pause, then a qualified yes | Usually a polite no | Downgrade |
| Immediate specific objection | **Engagement** | Positive signal about the person, neutral on the lead |

### Three standing rules

1. **When in doubt, score it against the idea.** You are the one with an
   incentive to be generous.
2. **A quote beats a rating.** If you record only one thing per card, record what
   they actually said.
3. **Count the "interesting"s.** Five interviews of warm interest with zero
   contacts made in 14 days is a KILL, whatever the tally says.
